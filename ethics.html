Our Ethics

The ethical framework behind every journey we design.

Dark tourism sits close to real trauma, real grief, and real communities. This is the code we hold ourselves to — and the reason a human specialist beats any algorithm or booking engine in this space.

01

Education Before Entertainment

Every itinerary is built to prioritise learning and remembrance over spectacle. If a stop exists only for a photograph, it doesn't belong on our itinerary.

02

Survivor and Descendant Respect

In destinations where trauma is recent — Rwanda, Bosnia, Cambodia — itineraries are built in consultation with local memorial organisations, not designed remotely from a spreadsheet.

03

No Sensation, No Selfie Culture

We brief every client on site etiquette before they travel: what to wear, how to behave, and what not to photograph. It is a core part of the service, not a footnote.

04

Legitimate Operators Only

Every local guide is vetted for accreditation and a genuine connection to the community. We do not resell generic city-tour packages under a darker name.

05

Psychological Preparation

You receive a pre-trip briefing document and a post-trip debrief, with counselling resources signposted where appropriate. These places affect people, and we don't pretend otherwise.

06

Economic Benefit to Communities

Wherever possible we choose locally-owned accommodation, guides, and restaurants, so the communities connected to these histories see a direct benefit from your visit.

Academic Grounding

Aligned with the Institute for Dark Tourism Research

Our framework draws on the work of the Institute for Dark Tourism Research (iDTR) at the University of Central Lancashire — the world's only dedicated academic centre for dark tourism scholarship, founded in 2012 by Dr Philip Stone and home to the International Journal of Dark Tourism Studies. We reference their published typologies and ethical frameworks directly in how we brief clients and vet destinations.

Dark tourism is not about voyeurism. Done well, it is education, remembrance, and an act of respect — and that distinction is the whole of our business.
